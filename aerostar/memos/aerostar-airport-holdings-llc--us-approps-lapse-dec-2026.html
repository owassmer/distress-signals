02 The company, and how the event reaches its revenue

The company and the revenue

Private operator of Luis Muñoz Marín International Airport (SJU/LMM), San Juan, Puerto Rico, under a 40-year lease from the Puerto Rico Ports Authority (from Feb 27, 2013). Earns a fixed, CPI-indexed annual contribution from signatory airlines under the Airport Use Agreements (US$76.8M in 2025), plus passenger-driven non-aeronautical revenue (duty free/retail, food and beverage, rental cars, parking and ground transportation) and other aeronautical fees; FY2025 total operating revenue US$219.6M on 6.8M enplanements.

The revenue this memo is about: Passenger-driven non-aeronautical revenue at Luis Muñoz Marín International Airport: concessions (duty free/retail, food & beverage, terminal services), rental car concessions and parking/ground transportation

Owners and counterparties: 60% owned by Grupo Aeroportuario del Sureste (ASUR, NYSE: ASR) through Aeropuerto de Cancún; 40% held by other shareholders. Lessor and revenue-share recipient: Puerto Rico Ports Authority (PRPA).

How the event reaches the revenue

A federal funding lapse leaves FAA air traffic controllers and TSA screeners working unpaid; if strain builds, the FAA can order airlines to cut domestic operations at High Impact Airports, as it did on Nov 7, 2025. About 91% of SJU's scheduled domestic passengers fly to those 40 airports, so cuts there remove SJU flights and passengers. Aerostar's airline fees are a fixed annual contribution under the Airport Use Agreements and do not move with traffic, but its concession, rental car and parking revenue is earned per passenger, so lost trips reduce it directly.

When

The CR in P.L. 119-103 expires end of day Dec 11, 2026; a lapse would start Dec 12, 2026, at the opening of SJU's Dec–Mar peak. In 2025 the FAA order came about five weeks into the lapse and was rolled back within days of funding being restored. Lost trips in a peak season are largely not made up later in the period.

What establishes this

  • Airline aeronautical revenue is a fixed, CPI-indexed annual contribution under the Airport Use Agreements, so it is not the traffic-exposed stream; the exposed stream is passenger-driven commercial revenue[4a]
  • Aerostar earns the concession, parking and rental car revenue at LMM[4b]
  • The 2025 lapse led the FAA to cut flights at 40 airports that are the destinations of most SJU domestic flights[3c]
  • ASUR reports San Juan commercial revenue on a per-passenger basis (Ps.163.3 per passenger in 3Q26), so it scales with passengers[5]

03 The event, and the ways it can turn out

Federal funding lapses Dec 12, 2026
Decided · On or by December 12, 2026

A lapse in federal appropriations begins on December 12, 2026: the continuing appropriations in Division A of P.L. 119-103 (Continuing Appropriations and Extensions Act, 2027) expire at the end of December 11, 2026 without a further continuing resolution or regular FY2027 appropriations acts having been enacted for every agency the CR funds, so that one or more federal agencies are without appropriations on December 12, 2026.

Decided by Congress and the President: whether a further CR, an omnibus/minibus, or the regular FY2027 bills are enacted before the CR expires at the end of December 11, 2026. As of mid-November 2026 none of the 12 regular bills was enacted, leaders had no agreed strategy, Democrats were discussing a CR into March or April 2027, and disputes over Iran war funding and a new $50 billion air-traffic-control request complicated a deal. CR expires end of day Dec 11, 2026 (fixed by statute); a lapse, if any, starts Dec 12, 2026.[1][2a][2b][2c]

Shown byCongress.gov public laws of the 119th Congress (any law enacted on or before December 11, 2026 extending appropriations), and OMB/agency lapse notices
FAA orders flight cuts during the lapse
True only with it · Decided · December 12, 2026 – March 31, 2027

During a lapse in appropriations that began on December 12, 2026, the FAA issues an emergency order (of the kind it issued on November 7, 2025) requiring air carriers to reduce scheduled operations at one or more U.S. airports because of air traffic controller staffing strain from the lapse, and the order is in effect at some time between December 12, 2026 and March 31, 2027.

Decided by the FAA Administrator under 49 U.S.C. 40103 once controller absences and safety reports rise during a lapse. In 2025 the order came on day 38 of a lapse that began Oct 1, cut domestic operations at 40 High Impact Airports by 4% rising toward 10%, was eased to 6% on Nov 13, and was rolled back after funding was restored Nov 12. Can only arise after a lapse begins Dec 12, 2026; in 2025 it took about five weeks of unpaid controller work.[3a][3b]

Shown byFederal Register (FAA emergency orders establishing operating limitations on the use of navigable airspace) and FAA newsroom

Appendix · what the memo rests on

A Findings recorded on the case

inference

The Oct 1–Nov 12, 2025 federal funding lapse, including the FAA's Nov 7–16 order cutting domestic operations at 40 High Impact Airports, left no visible mark on SJU traffic outside Spirit's own shrinkage. Non-Spirit domestic enplanements were −0.23% YoY in Sep 2025, −0.56% in Oct and +0.68% in Nov. SJU is not itself a High Impact Airport, but about 91% of its scheduled domestic passengers fly to those 40 airports, so a longer or peak-season order would reach it. That is why the lapse case carries only small traffic-loss values (0.2% without an FAA order, 0.7% with one) over Dec 12, 2026–Mar 31, 2027, a worst-world shortfall of about $0.23M.[C1][C2]
